The four cases — what they actually do

Greek marks a noun's job in the sentence by changing its ending and its article. English does this with word order ("the dog bites the man"); Greek does it with endings, which is why Greek word order can move around freely without changing the meaning.

Full endings for every noun type are on page 4. This section is only about what each case is for.

Quick reference

CaseGreek nameWhat it marksAnswersExample
NominativeΟνομαστικήthe subject — who or what does itΠοιος; Τι;Ο Γιάννης διαβάζει. — John is reading.
AccusativeΑιτιατικήthe direct object, and anything after a prepositionΠοιον; Τι;Βλέπω τον Γιάννη. — I see John.
GenitiveΓενικήpossession, and the person something is given toΤίνος;Το σπίτι του Γιάννη — John's house
VocativeΚλητικήcalling someone—Γιάννη, έλα! — John, come!

1 · Nominative — the doer

UseExampleEnglish
Subject of the verbΟ σκύλος τρώει.The dog is eating.
Subject (plural)Τα παιδιά παίζουν.The children are playing.
After είμαι ⚠Ο πατέρας μου είναι γιατρός.My father is a doctor.
After γίνομαι ⚠Έγινε δάσκαλος.He became a teacher.

⚠ This is the form dictionaries list. ⚠ After είμαι / γίνομαι the word stays nominative — both sides of the sentence are subjects, so nothing changes: Αυτός είναι ο Γιάννης.

2 · Accusative — the receiver, and after every preposition

UseExampleEnglish
Direct objectΑγόρασα ένα βιβλίο.I bought a book.
Direct objectΠεριμένω τον αδερφό μου.I'm waiting for my brother.
After σεΠάω στο σχολείο.I go to school.
After απόΉρθα από τη δουλειά.I came from work.
After μεΜιλάω με τη Μαρία.I'm talking to Maria.
After γιαΕίναι για σένα.It's for you.
How longΈμεινα τρεις μέρες.I stayed three days.

⚠ Every common preposition takes the accusative — σε, από, με, για, χωρίς, μέχρι, προς, μετά, πριν, κατά. There is nothing to choose: after a preposition, accusative.

⚠ Some Greek verbs take a plain accusative where English needs a preposition:

GreekEnglish
περιμένω τον Γιάννηwait for John
ψάχνω τα κλειδιά μουlook for my keys
κοιτάζω τη θάλασσαlook at the sea
πληρώνω τον λογαριασμόpay for the bill

3 · Genitive — "of", "'s", and "to someone"

UseExampleEnglish
PossessionΤο σπίτι του ΓιάννηJohn's house
PossessionΗ πόρτα του σπιτιούthe door of the house
Possession (plural)Η μητέρα των παιδιώνthe children's mother
Indirect objectΔίνω του Γιάννη ένα δώρο.I give John a present.
Possessive pronounτο σπίτι μουmy house

⚠ Greek has no dative case. "To / for someone" is either the genitive or σε + accusative, and in everyday speech σε + accusative wins:

Genitiveσε + accusative (more common)
Δίνω της Μαρίας το βιβλίο.Δίνω το βιβλίο στη Μαρία.
Λέω του παιδιού την αλήθεια.Λέω την αλήθεια στο παιδί.

⚠ But with weak pronouns the genitive is the normal choice: Μου έδωσε το βιβλίο (he gave me the book). Those little words — μου, σου, του, της, μας, σας, τους — are the genitive.

⚠ Word order: the genitive comes after the thing owned — το σπίτι του Γιάννη, never the reverse.

4 · Vocative — calling someone

Used only when you speak to a person directly. It never takes an article.

NominativeVocativeNote
ο ΓιάννηςΓιάννη!-ης → -η
ο ΠέτροςΠέτρο!names in -ος → -ο
ο κύριοςΚύριε!common nouns in -ος → -ε
ο φίλοςΦίλε!
ο γιατρόςΓιατρέ!
η ΜαρίαΜαρία!feminine unchanged
το παιδίΠαιδί!neuter unchanged
τα παιδιάΠαιδιά!very common

⚠ Never put the article in front: Γιάννη, έλα! — not Ο Γιάννη, έλα!

Why the endings matter: word order can move

GreekEnglish
Ο Γιάννης βλέπει τη Μαρία.John sees Maria.neutral order
Τη Μαρία βλέπει ο Γιάννης.John sees Maria.same meaning, Maria emphasised

ο Γιάννης is nominative and τη Μαρία accusative, so you always know who is doing the seeing — no matter which one comes first. In English, swapping the two would reverse the meaning.

Which case do I need?

Ask yourselfCase
Is it doing the action?Nominative
Does it come after είμαι / γίνομαι?Nominative
Is it receiving the action directly?Accusative
Is there a preposition in front of it?Accusative, always
Does it mean "of…" or "…'s"?Genitive
Is it the person given / told something?Genitive, or σε + accusative
Am I calling this person?Vocative

In the HTML and PDF the gender bands are real cell backgrounds that fill the whole row.

Theme switch

Every HTML file (the combined one and each per-sheet file) has a pill button in the top-right corner that cycles:

LabelBehaviour
Autofollows the OS / browser setting via prefers-color-scheme (the default)
Lightpinned light, ignores the OS setting
Darkpinned dark, ignores the OS setting

The choice is stored in localStorage under greek-a2-theme and survives reloads. A tiny script in <head> applies it before first paint, so a pinned dark theme does not flash white. Dark mode uses deeper gender bands with light text rather than the pastel ones.

Why color-scheme is written the way it is

Do not "simplify" these three declarations:

SelectorValueReason
:rootlight darkmust advertise dark support at all times
:root[data-theme="light"]only lightexplicit opt-out of forced darkening
:root[data-theme="dark"]only darksame, for the pinned dark theme

Chrome's Auto Dark Mode for Web Contents (chrome://flags/#enable-force-dark) inverts any page whose root resolves to light-only. With plain color-scheme: light the Light setting was silently inverted back to a black background, so the toggle appeared to do nothing but nudge the text colour. The only keyword is the documented opt-out. Verified by rendering each state with --enable-features=WebContentsForceDark and confirming the output is byte-identical to a normal render.

⚠ The PDF is always light, whatever theme is selected — the print stylesheet resets the palette. Worth knowing because headless Chrome reports prefers-color-scheme: dark, so without that reset the PDF would have printed dark.
