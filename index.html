5. The final -ν rule

τον, την, έναν, δεν, μην keep -ν before a vowel or before κ π τ, μπ ντ γκ, τσ τζ, ξ ψ. They drop it before the other consonants (β γ δ ζ θ λ μ ν ρ σ φ χ).

Keeps -νDrops -ν

την πόρτατη θάλασσα
τον Αντρέατη δουλειά
την τσάντατη μαμά
έναν μπακλαβάτη ρόδα

⚠ In practice the masculine τον is almost always written in full — it is what distinguishes masculine from neuter (τον δρόμο vs το δρόμο). ⚠ The feminine την → τη freely before β γ δ ζ θ λ μ ν ρ σ φ χ: τη θάλασσα, τη Δευτέρα.

03 · The four cases (Πτώσεις) — what they actually do

Greek marks a noun's job in the sentence by changing its ending and its article. English does this with word order ("the dog bites the man"); Greek does it with endings, which is why Greek word order can move around freely without changing the meaning.

The endings themselves are on page 4. This sheet is about what each case is for — what those four column headings actually mean, and which question word asks for which one.

Quick reference

NominativeΟνομαστικήthe subject — who or what does itΠοιος; Τι;Ο Γιάννης διαβάζει. — John is reading.

⚠ Greek has no dative case. "To / for someone" is either the genitive or σε + accusative, and in everyday speech σε + accusative wins:

Genitiveσε + accusative (more common)
Δίνω της Μαρίας το βιβλίο.Δίνω το βιβλίο στη Μαρία.
Λέω του παιδιού την αλήθεια.Λέω την αλήθεια στο παιδί.

⚠ But with weak pronouns the genitive is the normal choice: Μου έδωσε το βιβλίο (he gave me the book). Those little words — μου, σου, του, της, μας, σας, τους — are the genitive.

⚠ Word order: the genitive comes after the thing owned — το σπίτι του Γιάννη, never the reverse.

0. The big idea: Greek has no infinitive

μπορείς να έρθεις;   = can you come?
πρέπει να φύγουμε    = we must leave

19 · The verb είμαι (to be)

The most common verb in Greek, and the one that behaves least like the others. Learn it as its own thing — the patterns on pages 10–13 mostly do not apply to it.

What makes it different ⚠

have an aorist (έγραψα)no aorist — one past tense only
have a dependent form (να γράψω)no dependent form
have two futures (θα γράψω / θα γράφω)one future only
have a perfect (έχω γράψει)no perfect
take an object in the accusativetakes a nominative after it

6. ⚠ What follows είμαι stays NOMINATIVE

This is the rule that catches people out. είμαι does not take an object — the word after it is another subject, so it keeps the nominative, and adjectives agree with the subject.

GreekEnglish
Ο πατέρας μου είναι γιατρός.My father is a doctor.noun, nominative
Η Μαρία είναι κουρασμένη.Maria is tired.feminine adjective
Τα παιδιά είναι χαρούμενα.The children are happy.neuter plural
Αυτός είναι ο Γιάννης.That's John.nominative both sides
Είμαι Ρώσος / Ρωσίδα.I'm Russian (man / woman).matches the speaker

⚠ Compare with a normal verb, which does take the accusative: Βλέπω τον γιατρό (accusative) vs *Είμαι γιατρός*** (nominative).
